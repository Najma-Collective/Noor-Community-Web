Land Maps as Trauma-Sensitive Warm Ups

We open every session by drawing the ground beneath us so nervous systems know they are safe.

In our Beirut satellite studio, facilitators noticed that students arrived jumpy after long commutes. Mapping the land together became a grounding ritual.

Category
Pedagogical Insights
Published
April 30, 2024
Authored by
Farah Haddad, Studio Lead

This facilitator insight sits inside our English for Academic Purposes toolkit, offering concrete moves that keep language objectives visible while delivering hands-on content.

How the practice works

Students receive translucent paper and are asked to sketch everything beneath their feet: the tiles, soil, bus floor, or rooftop membrane. They label textures ("cool", "gritty") and note the people who share the ground with them. Facilitators layer the sheets to reveal a collective land map.

Why it matters

The routine signals that every learner belongs to the land, not to a single classroom. It slows breathing, prompts sensory detail, and surfaces land acknowledgments that are meaningful, not performative. Students now initiate the ritual themselves when someone joins late.

Adaptations

  • For online pods, students trace their hands and write the land story on each finger.
  • Younger learners use clay to sculpt the ground and then press it onto paper to capture texture.
  • Facilitators end with a question: “What does the land need from us today?” which guides the lesson plan.
